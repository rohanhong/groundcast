# S03 Effective learning techniques (Dunlosky et al. 2013)

- Source: Dunlosky, J., Rawson, K.A., Marsh, E.J., Nathan, M.J. and Willingham, D.T. 2013. Improving Students' Learning With Effective Learning Techniques: Promising Directions From Cognitive and Educational Psychology. *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 14(1), 4-58. doi:10.1177/1529100612453266
- Read: 2026-10-12; introduction and Table 2 (pp. 4-7), sections 1 (elaborative interrogation, pp. 8-11), 2.4-2.5 (self-explanation), 7.4-7.5 (rereading, pp. 29), 8 (practice testing, pp. 29-35), 9 (distributed practice, pp. 35-39), 10.4-10.5 and Closing Remarks (pp. 44-45). ## Candidate rules

| Rule (our words) | Locator | Role | Pass example (ours) | Fail example (ours) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Retrieval beats re-exposure. Keep recaps short and spend the saved time on recall questions: hearing a point again is a weak study method, answering a question about it is a strong one. | 7.4-7.5, p. 29 (rereading rated low, inferior to practice testing); 8.5, p. 35 | Writer (recall) | Segment recap: "So the lake fills from two streams." Recall segment then asks: "Which two streams fill Lake Marrow?" | A closing recap that restates every sentence of the session in new words, and no recall question. |
| Every recall question is followed at once by its correct answer. Testing with feedback is reliably better than testing alone and protects against a wrong guess sticking. | 8.4, pp. 34-35 | Writer (recall) | "What drains Lake Marrow?" then "The Askra, its only outlet." | "What drains Lake Marrow? Keep that in mind; we'll come back to it next time." |
| Recall questions ask the listener to produce the answer, not to pick or confirm it. Free recall and short answers help more than recognition. No yes/no or either/or questions. | 8.2a, p. 30 (more generative formats beat fill-in and recognition) | Writer (recall) | "What happens to the lake level in a dry spring?" | "Does the lake level fall in a dry spring, yes or no?" / "Is the outlet the Pell or the Askra?" |
| Space retrieval. A question asked straight after the point is taught does little; ask it after other material has intervened. Within a session the end-of-session Recall segment does this; a question about segment 1 is better placed after segment 2 than at the end of segment 1. | 8.2a, pp. 30-31 (repeated tests in immediate succession add little; longer lags help); 9.1-9.2, pp. 36-37 | Writer (recall) | Session order: segment 1 inflow, segment 2 outflow, Recall: inflow question first. | Each segment ends with a recall question on the sentence just spoken. |
| More successful retrievals, spread over sessions, give better retention, with diminishing returns. Quizzes should bring back a concept in later sessions until the learner has answered it correctly more than once. | 8.2a, p. 30 (dosage); 8.4, pp. 34-35; 9.5, p. 39 | Quiz design (scheduling) | A concept answered wrong today returns in the next quiz; one answered right twice on different days is retired. | Each quiz covers only the session just heard and never repeats a concept. |
| A multiple-choice quiz shows the correct answer and a short explanation after each question. Without feedback, learners can later recall a wrong option as true. Feedback may come at the end of the quiz. | 8.4, pp. 34-35 (wrong alternatives intrude without feedback; delayed feedback works) | Quiz writer | After the answer: "Correct answer: the Askra. The notes say the Askra is the only stream that leaves the lake." | The quiz shows only a score, so a learner who picked "the Pell" never learns it was wrong. |
| Why and how questions help when the learner has some knowledge of the topic. Ask them only about causes or mechanisms the session actually explained, so the answer can be given from the session. | 1.1, p. 8; 1.5, p. 11 (moderate utility; weaker for low prior knowledge); Table 2 discussion, p. 7 | Writer (recall); Quiz writer | The session explained that snowmelt raises the Pell in May; recall asks: "Why does the Pell run high in May?" | "Why do lakes form?" when the session never covered lake formation. |
| Practice questions that need a small inference from taught facts also help later understanding, so a quiz may ask what follows from two taught points, as long as the passages state the conclusion. | 8.2d, pp. 33-34 | Quiz writer | Taught: the Askra is the only outlet; a dam closed the Askra. Question: "What happened to the outflow after the dam closed?" (the passages state the outflow stopped). | A question whose answer the learner must infer and the passages never state. |
| Mixing questions on different topics helps learners tell similar ideas apart; it is rated moderate and mostly shown for problem types. Order quiz questions so neighbouring questions come from different segments when the segments cover easily confused ideas. | 10.4-10.5, p. 44 | Quiz writer | Inflow, outflow, inflow, outflow questions alternate. | All inflow questions, then all outflow questions. |

## Not adopted

- Summarization, highlighting, keyword mnemonic, and imagery for text: rated low utility, and they are learner reading techniques with no direct use in a listened script.
- Self-explanation prompts inside the script ("explain to yourself why ..."): moderate utility but costly in time, and the answer cannot be checked; recall questions with given answers cover the need.
- A fixed spacing interval: the best gap depends on how long the learner must remember (9.2a); groundcast has no retention target yet, so scheduling stays a later decision.
- Interleaving as a hard rule: the evidence is small and mostly mathematics (10.5); kept as a weak ordering preference only.
