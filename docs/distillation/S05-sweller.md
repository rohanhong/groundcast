# S05 Cognitive load theory, 20 years later (Sweller et al. 2019)

- Source: Sweller, J., van Merrienboer, J.J.G. and Paas, F. 2019. Cognitive Architecture and Instructional Design: 20 Years Later. *Educational Psychology Review* 31(2), 261-292. doi:10.1007/s10648-019-09465-5 (open access, CC BY)
- Read: 2026-10-12; Short History, Categories of Cognitive Load (pp. 262-265), the 1998 effects incl. redundancy and modality (pp. 266-269), Table 1 and the new compound effects: element interactivity, expertise reversal, guidance fading, transient information, self-explanation, imagination, isolated elements, collective working memory (pp. 276-280).

## Candidate rules

| Rule (our words) | Locator | Role | Pass example (ours) | Fail example (ours) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Speech is transient: each sentence is gone once spoken, so the listener must hold earlier parts in mind. Keep each stretch of new material short and close it with a recap before the next one starts. Longer spoken stretches can lose the advantage that speech otherwise has. | Transient Information Effect, pp. 277-278 (Leahy and Sweller: short audio helps, long audio hurts) | Writer | A segment explains the Pell's spring rise in five sentences and ends: "So: snowmelt, then a high Pell." | One segment covers inflow, outflow, seasons, and fish in thirty sentences with no recap. |
| Segmenting with pauses helps novices most. Put a clearly longer pause at every segment boundary than between sentences. | Transient Information Effect, p. 278 (segmentation effect; not found for high prior knowledge) | Voice director | Half a second between sentences, about two seconds between segments. | The same short gap everywhere, so segments run together. |
| Letting learners control pace offsets transience. Segments are the units for navigation: headings become chapter markers, and the player can pause, go back one sentence, and jump to a segment. | Transient Information Effect, pp. 277-278 (self-pacing effect) | App (player); Writer (headings) | Heading "Where the water leaves" is a chapter the learner can replay. | One continuous track with no markers. |
| Introduce elements on their own before explaining how they interact. Name and define each part first, then the relation. This lowers the load of material with many interacting parts. | Isolated Elements Effect, p. 279; element interactivity, pp. 263-264 | Writer | "The Pell flows in. The Askra flows out. Now, how the two together set the lake level." | "The lake level depends on the balance between the Pell's snowmelt-driven inflow and the Askra's dam-limited outflow," as the first mention of either stream. |
| What is hard depends on what the learner already knows. Support that helps a beginner, such as extra definitions and repetition, can slow down a learner who knows the terms. Scale definitions and recaps to the learner's level given in the brief or profile. | Expertise Reversal Effect and Guidance-Fading Effect, p. 277; intrinsic load, p. 264 | Writer | Brief says the learner knows the basic terms: the session skips "An inflow is a stream that feeds a lake." | Re-defining "inflow" in every session for a learner whose profile marks it as known. |
| Redundant information costs effort: the learner has to work out that two versions say the same thing. Inside a segment, do not restate a point in new words right after stating it. Repetition belongs in planned places: segment recaps, closing recap, recall answers. | Redundancy Effect, pp. 268-269 | Writer; Editor | "The Askra is the only outlet." Next sentence adds new information. | "The Askra is the only outlet. In other words, water leaves only by the Askra. That is, the Askra alone drains it." |
| Prompts to explain or recall help only when the total load stays within capacity. Do not put a recall or reflection prompt in the middle of a multi-step explanation; place it after the segment's recap or in the Recall segment. | Self-Explanation Effect, p. 278 | Writer (recall) | Steps one to three of the spring flood, recap, then later the question. | "Before step three, can you guess what happens next?" in the middle of the chain. |
| Asking the listener to picture or mentally rehearse a process helps only once they can hold it in mind; for new material, explaining works better than imagining. Use "picture this" framing after the process has been explained, or for learners who know it. | Imagination Effect, p. 279 | Writer | After explaining the flood steps: "Picture the Pell rising over a week as the snow goes." | Opening a new topic with "Imagine everything that controls this lake's level." |

## Not adopted

- Worked example, goal-free, and completion-problem effects: they concern learning to solve problems, which a listened session does not practise.
- Modality effect as a reason for audio-only delivery: it concerns spoken words paired with diagrams; groundcast has no diagrams, so the effect gives no rule beyond the transient-information limits above. See S25 for the transcript display question.
- Collective working memory: it concerns learners collaborating; two synthetic hosts are not two learners.
- Human movement effect and cognitive load measurement: not relevant to scripts.
- A number for maximum segment length: the source gives none; set it from evaluation, not from this note.
