Local copies: `S07-npr-campfire-tales.html`, `S07-npr-would-you-say-it.html`, `S07-npr-writing-through-sound.html`, `S07-npr-inskeep-three-songs.html`.
- Read: 2026-10-12; all four pages in full. Locators name the page and its section heading or list item. "Tape" and "act" in these pages mean recorded speech by someone other than the narrator; for groundcast the nearest match is the other host's line.

## Candidate rules

| Rule (our words) | Locator | Role | Pass example (ours) | Fail example (ours) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A session has one main point, or two closely linked ones. If the session cannot be summed up in one sentence, it is not focused enough. Say that sentence near the start. | Campfire tales, "It starts with a clear focus" | Writer | "Today: how two streams keep Lake Marrow's level steady." | A session that drifts from the lake level to fish species to the boating club with no one-sentence summary possible. |
| Make a point, then move on; do not circle back to it in the middle of later material. Repetition is kept for the planned recaps (T1 sources S03, S05 require them; this rule governs only the body of a segment). | Campfire tales, "Think about rhythm and flow" (do not double back) | Writer; Editor | Segment 2 on the Askra does not re-explain the Pell; the closing recap names both. | Mid-way through the Askra segment: "As I said, the Pell flows in, which is important, because the Pell flows in." |
| Raise a question in the listener's mind, then answer it. Open a segment with the question that segment answers. | Campfire tales, "Think about rhythm and flow" (raise a question) | Writer | "So if two streams flow in, why doesn't the lake overflow every spring?" then the answer. | Listing the facts about the outlet with no question to pull the listener forward. |
| Signposts are essential between topics: one or two sentences that say the last part is done and a different part begins. Radio cannot jump as print can. | Campfire tales, "Signposts are critical"; Writing through sound, item 4 (Signpost: step back, transition, review) | Writer | "That's how water gets in. Now for the harder question: how it gets out." | The last inflow sentence is followed directly by "The Askra is twelve kilometres long." |
| Short sentences with few dependent clauses, because speech has simpler syntax than print and the listener cannot go back. Do not open a sentence with a long clause before the subject. | Campfire tales, "Use short sentences"; Would you say it, "Beware clauses" and "Keep your sentences short and simple" | Writer; Editor | "The lake is fed by two streams. Both start in the hills." | "Fed by two streams that both rise in the northern hills, the lake, which is shallow, ..." |
| Prefer verbs to adjectives, and active verbs to passive ones. | Campfire tales, "Verbs are better than adjectives"; Inskeep, "Thunder Road" takeaway | Editor | "Snowmelt swells the Pell in May." | "In May, a significant increase in the Pell's volume is caused by snowmelt." |
| Keep the same word for the same thing. Switching to a synonym to avoid repetition sounds odd and, for a learner, can suggest a second thing. | Would you say it, "Repeating words is not always bad" | Editor; Writer | "The Askra drains the lake. The Askra also carries silt." | "The Askra drains the lake. The outflow channel also carries silt." (Is the channel the Askra?) |
| Use words you would use in conversation, and be plain rather than poetic: clarity wins on radio. | Would you say it, "Don't use words or phrases you wouldn't use in normal conversation" and "Be straightforward" | Editor | "It's the lake's biggest flood on record." | "The lake experienced an unprecedented hydrological event of record-breaking magnitude." |
| Analogies help with technical ideas but use them sparingly, one at a time, and never mix two images in one sentence. | Campfire tales, metaphors and similes items | Writer (framing) | "Think of the Askra as the lake's drain." | "The Askra is the lake's drain, a safety valve that keeps the engine from boiling over." |
| Read the script aloud: a sentence that sounds fine in the head but trips the tongue, or needs a breath in the middle, is written for print. | Campfire tales, "How to edit yourself"; Would you say it (reading the print example aloud) | Editor; Voice director | "The lake rose fast. It peaked in June." | A 35-word sentence with three numbers that cannot be said in one breath. |
| Vary the length of sentences and of segments; equal lengths throughout sound machine-made. | Campfire tales, "Vary lengths of acts and tracks" | Writer; Voice director | Short and medium sentences mixed; a short segment after a long one. | Every sentence 12 words, every segment six sentences. |
| A question line must not contain its answer, and a lead-in must not say what the next line will say. | Campfire tales, "Don't step on your acts"; Writing through sound, item 3 (question then answer) | Writer (dialogue) | A: "So where does the water go?" B: "Out through the Askra." | A: "So the water leaves through the Askra. Where does it go?" B: "Through the Askra." |
| Ways to link two voices: echo a key word from the previous line and build on it, react briefly to a striking point, or take the next step. Each linked line must still be a complete sentence that makes sense after only the line before it. | Writing through sound, part 2 items 2 (React), 3 (Echo), 4 (Take the next step) | Writer (dialogue) | B: "Twelve kilometres?" A: "Twelve kilometres, and all of it downhill." | A line that only works as the second half of a sentence: A: "The level falls when ..." B: "... the dam opens." |
| Start from a specific case, not a general statement, when the passages give one, and let it point to the general idea. | Inskeep, "Busted" takeaway and final takeaway | Writer | "In the spring of the big flood, the Pell rose two metres in a week. That rise shows how snowmelt drives the lake." (passages give the case) | "Many factors influence lake levels in various ways." as the opening. |
| Direct address works when used sparingly, for example a sentence opening "Now," to turn the listener's attention. | Campfire tales, "You can address the listener in a direct way" | Writer | One "Now, here's the surprise." per segment at most. | "Now," opening five sentences in a row. |

## Not adopted

- Ambient sound, tease and back-ID, butt cuts, montage, and "let the sound come in first" (Campfire tales, Writing through sound): groundcast has no recorded tape or sound design; voices are synthetic.
- Avoid "ping-pong" between speakers (Campfire tales): written for many interviewees; groundcast's two-host format switches hosts often by design (dialogue.md).
- "Write to tape" and keeping the reporter's questions in: about field recording.
- Sentence fragments (Campfire tales): allowed by NPR, but each groundcast sentence is checked on its own; a fragment that carries a fact cannot be verified. Fragments only as connective sentences.
- Humour and reactions with snark (Writing through sound, The Hint and React): tone, not checkable; the Verifier would have to judge implied claims.
- Ending a story before its climax (Inskeep, "Tom Ames' Prayer"): conflicts with the closing recap required by T1 sources.
