# S25 Cognitive theory of multimedia learning, review (Mayer 2024)

- Source: Mayer, R.E. 2024. The Past, Present, and Future of the Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning. *Educational Psychology Review* 36, article 8. doi:10.1007/s10648-023-09842-1 (open access, CC BY 4.0). Open substitute for S1 (Mayer, *Multimedia Learning*, 3rd ed.): written by the same author, it summarises the book's theory and its 15 design principles (Table 6 is taken from Mayer 2021).
- Read: 2026-10-12; the whole essay, closely: Current State (guiding assumptions, memory stores, three demands on capacity, three instructional goals, pp. 16-19 of 25) and Table 6 (p. 20 of 25). Locators use the article's "Page n of 25".

## Candidate rules

| Rule (our words) | Locator | Role | Pass example (ours) | Fail example (ours) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Coherence: leave out material that does not serve the session's goal, even when it is interesting and even when it is in the passages. A side story competes with the key points for the same small capacity. | Table 6, principle 1, p. 20; Three Instructional Goals, p. 18 | Writer | A session on the lake's water budget skips the passage about the 1950s boating club. | Two sentences on the boating club's history in the middle of the inflow segment. |
| Signaling: add cues that show how the essential material is organised: an opening outline with the number of parts, an ordinal signpost at each part, and a closing recap in the same order. | Table 6, principle 2, p. 20 | Writer | "Three things set the lake level. First, the Pell ... Second, the Askra ... Third, evaporation." | Moving from the Pell to evaporation with no signpost and no outline. |
| Pretraining: learners follow an explanation better when they already know the names and main traits of its key parts. Before a causal or step-by-step explanation, give a short segment that names each key part with one defining trait. | Table 6, principle 7, p. 20 | Writer | Segment 1: "Two streams matter here: the Pell, which flows in, and the Askra, which flows out." Segment 2 explains how they set the level. | The flood mechanism is explained while the names Pell and Askra are introduced in passing. |
| Segmenting: a lesson in learner-paced segments beats one continuous stream. Keep segments as navigable units (see S05). | Table 6, principle 6, p. 20; essential processing, p. 19 | Writer; App (player) | Each segment has a heading and can be replayed alone. | A single segment titled "Lake Marrow" for the whole session. |
| Personalization: conversational style beats formal style. Address the listener as "you", use "we" for shared steps, and prefer spoken phrasing over textbook phrasing. | Table 6, principle 10, p. 20 | Writer; Editor | "Here's what you'll notice: the lake rises before the rain does." | "It may be observed that the lake level increase precedes precipitation events." |
| Voice: narration in a friendly, human-sounding voice beats a machine-sounding one. Choose natural voices and pacing; a voice that sounds robotic is a learning cost, not just a style issue. | Table 6, principle 11, p. 20 | Voice director | A natural voice at an even conversational pace. | A flat synthetic voice at maximum speed to shorten the session. |
| Generative activity: guided activities during learning, such as self-testing in pauses, foster deeper processing. Recall prompts with a pause before the answer are the audio form of this. | Table 6, principle 15, p. 20; Three Instructional Goals, p. 19 | Writer (recall) | "Which stream drains the lake?" pause, then the answer. | Ending with "Think about what you learned today" and no question. |
| Redundancy: adding printed text to narration does not help when the lesson is fast-paced. When the app plays a session, the full transcript should not be shown in sync by default; offer it on demand. | Table 6, principle 3, p. 20 | App (web UI) | Transcript hidden during playback, one click to show it. | Every sentence highlighted on screen as it is read. |
| Capacity is small and processing is active: only a few pieces of information can be handled at once, and learners must select, organise, and connect them. One new idea per sentence and one main idea per segment follow from this. | Guiding Assumptions and Three Memory Stores, pp. 17-18 | Writer | "The Pell rises in May. That rise comes from snowmelt." | "The Pell, which rises in May because of snowmelt from the northern ridge that also feeds the Askra's upper branch, sets the level." |

## Not adopted

- Multimedia, spatial contiguity, temporal contiguity, image, embodiment, and immersion principles: they concern pictures, screens, onscreen agents, or VR; groundcast sessions are audio only. The multimedia principle (words plus pictures beat words alone) is a known limit of the audio-only design, recorded here rather than ignored.
- Effect sizes from Table 6: not copied into prompts; the principles are used as rules, and each has boundary conditions (for whom, for which lessons) that this short review does not detail (p. 19).
- Social, affective, and motivational extensions (p. 21): described as future work, so no rule is drawn from them yet.
