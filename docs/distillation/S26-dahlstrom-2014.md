# S26 Narratives in science communication (Dahlstrom 2014)

- Source: Dahlstrom, M.F. 2014. Using narratives and storytelling to communicate science with nonexpert audiences. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* 111(Suppl. 4), 13614-13620. doi:10.1073/pnas.1320645111 (free full text at PubMed Central, PMC4183170). Peer-reviewed open substitute for the narrative-structure principles of S9 (Abel, *Out on the Wire*), which has no openly hosted text.
- Read: 2026-10-12; whole article: Abstract, introduction, Narratives, Mass Media and Narratives, Narrative Persuasion and Ethical Considerations, Future Narrative Intersections. Locators use section headings.

## Candidate rules

| Rule (our words) | Locator | Role | Pass example (ours) | Fail example (ours) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A narrative (events in time order, linked by cause and effect, happening to someone or something) is easier to understand and remember than the same content as a list of facts. When the passages describe a process or a history, tell it in time order with each step causing the next. | Narratives (definition: causality, time order, character; studies of recall and reading speed) | Writer | "Snow melts in April. The Pell rises in May. The lake climbs in June." | "Factors: snowmelt; Pell discharge; lake level; seasonal timing." |
| Learning content that is part of the story's plot is learned better than content added on the side. A framing story must carry the key point, not decorate it. | Narratives (capacity model paragraph) | Writer (framing) | A framing story about a ranger reading the gauge each week, where the rising readings are the point being taught. | A story about the ranger's dog, followed by the fact about the gauge. |
| When a single case and an overall finding appear together, listeners' judgments drift toward the case. State the overall finding first, and say how typical the case is when the passages say so; never present one case as the rule. | Narrative Persuasion and Ethical Considerations (exemplification paragraph) | Writer | "Most years the lake rises less than a metre. The flood year, at two metres, was the exception." | Telling only the flood-year story, so the listener takes two metres as normal. |
| Choose representative examples over extreme ones unless the passages present the extreme case as such. | Narrative Persuasion and Ethical Considerations (second ethical question, representativeness) | Writer | Example of a typical spring. | The worst flood on record as the only example of spring. |
| Details in stories are absorbed as true even when the audience knows the story is invented, and accepted stories resist later correction. So invented framing must add no factual-sounding details (numbers, dates, names, causes) that the passages do not give. | Narrative Persuasion and Ethical Considerations (fiction and persuasion paragraphs) | Writer (framing); Verifier | Framing: "Picture a bathtub with the tap on and the plug out." (no new facts) | Framing: "Picture the old miller in 1890 watching the water rise three metres." (invented date and number sound like facts) |
| Framing may bend realism in its story elements (a personified stream, a talking fish) as long as the process it maps stays accurate. | Narrative Persuasion and Ethical Considerations (second ethical question, external realism; personified-yeast example) | Writer (framing) | "Think of the Askra as an impatient guest, leaving as fast as the Pell arrives." when the passages say outflow matches inflow. | The same image when the passages say the outflow lags the inflow by weeks. |
| The goal decides the use: groundcast uses narrative for understanding, not to steer the listener to a view. Where the passages present more than one view, a story must not favour one of them. | Narrative Persuasion and Ethical Considerations (first ethical question, persuasion versus comprehension) | Writer | A story that walks through both proposed dam plans the notes describe. | A story whose hero picks one plan and is rewarded, when the notes take no side. |

## Not adopted

- Narrative used for persuasion of resistant audiences: outside groundcast's purpose (explaining the learner's own materials).
- Long narrative arcs whose sentences only make sense together: the article notes narratives are hard to split into self-contained units, but each groundcast sentence is checked on its own, so every narrative sentence must still stand as a checkable claim or marked framing.
- Mass media, trust, and new media sections: background on science journalism, no script rule.
