The Water Cycle: Revision Notes

These notes were written for the groundcast evaluation set.

Evaporation

Water moves between the oceans, the air, and the land in a continuous cycle
driven by energy from the Sun. Evaporation turns liquid water into water
vapour. Most evaporation happens over the oceans, because they cover most of
the surface of the Earth. Warmer water evaporates faster than cold water.

Plants also release water vapour through small openings in their leaves. This
is called transpiration. Evaporation and transpiration together are often
called evapotranspiration.

Condensation

As moist air rises, it expands and cools. Cooler air can hold less water
vapour, so some of the vapour condenses into tiny droplets around particles
such as dust or salt. Billions of these droplets make up a cloud.

Precipitation

When droplets in a cloud collide and grow heavy enough, they fall as
precipitation. Precipitation can be rain, snow, sleet, or hail, depending on
the temperature of the air it falls through.

Collection

Precipitation that reaches the ground either soaks into the soil or runs off
over the surface. Water that soaks in is called infiltration; some of it moves
down to become groundwater, which can stay underground for a very long time.
Runoff flows into streams and rivers and eventually returns to the sea.

Storage

At any moment, most of the water on Earth is stored in the oceans. Most of the
fresh water is frozen in ice sheets and glaciers. Only a small share of fresh
water is in lakes and rivers.

Why it matters

The water cycle moves heat around the planet: evaporation takes up heat, and
condensation releases it into the air. This is one reason why the water cycle
affects weather.
