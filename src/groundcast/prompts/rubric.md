## Script rubric

Correctness outranks every other rule. Then logical structure, then fluency,
then vividness. When a rule for holding attention conflicts with a rule for
learning, learning wins.

Correctness
- Every sentence that says something about the subject is a claim and cites
  the passage IDs it rests on. Cite only passages that state it.
- Say nothing the passages do not say. Do not fill gaps from general
  knowledge, even when you are confident. Keep numbers, names, dates, units,
  and qualifiers ("often", "in most cases") exactly as the passages give them.
- One fact per sentence where possible, so each sentence can be checked on
  its own.
- When the passages give a finding's limits or how strong its evidence is
  (one study, small, mixed), say so in words in the same segment as the
  finding.
- Give the overall finding before a single case, and say how typical the
  case is when the passages say so. Never present one case as the rule.
- An analogy or example that is not in the passages is framing. Mark it as
  framing, keep it short, and make sure the mapping it suggests is true to the
  passages. Framing adds no facts of its own: no numbers, dates, names, or
  causes the passages do not give.

Structure
- Open with the question the session answers, or with a concrete case from
  the passages that raises it. Then say in a sentence why it matters, and
  give the plan: how many parts, in what order.
- Before explaining how things relate, name each key part with one defining
  trait. Define a term in one plain sentence before using it; define paired
  terms side by side ("An inflow brings water in. An outflow takes it out.").
- One segment per main idea. Its heading is the question it answers, and the
  segment answers it by its end. Tell a process or a history in time order,
  each step leading to the next.
- End each segment with a one-sentence recap and, except in the last, one
  sentence naming the next question. Close by answering the opening question
  directly, with its conditions, and tie the parts together. Keep recaps
  short; the planned recaps are the only places to repeat a point.
- Leave out material that does not serve the brief's focus, even when it is
  in the passages. Do not restate a point in other words right after making
  it.
- Fit the brief's level: more definitions and recaps for introductory
  listeners, fewer for advanced ones.
- To refer back to an earlier point, name it again ("Remember the Pell, the
  stream that brings the snowmelt in?"), never "as we said".

Writing for the ear
- Short sentences, mostly under 20 words; one idea per sentence. Put the
  subject and verb early; never open with a long clause.
- Use signposts: "First", "Here is the key point", "Now, the second step".
- Say it the way a person would say it aloud: contractions are fine, "you"
  is fine. Prefer active verbs to passive ones and to strings of adjectives.
- No tables, lists, parentheses, footnotes, symbols, or abbreviations that
  only work on the page. Write numbers and units the way they are spoken
  ("about three quarters", "twelve kilometres").
- Put attribution before the statement: "According to the notes, ...". Name
  a source by what it is before what it says.
- Keep the same word for the same thing all the way through; a new word
  suggests a new thing. Vary sentence openings and lengths instead, and
  avoid filler and hype.

Vividness
- Raise a question in the listener's mind, then answer it. Where the
  passages answer a likely objection, voice the objection, then answer it.
- Use a concrete case from the passages where one exists, and prefer a
  typical example to an extreme one.
- Announce an analogy or invented example as one ("Think of it as ...",
  "Imagine ..."), right after the idea it illustrates, one image at a time,
  in one or two sentences; then return to the passages. Ask the listener to
  picture a process only after it has been explained.
